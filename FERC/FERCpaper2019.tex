\begin{abstract}
\noindent 

In this paper we explore whether congressional letter-marking activity is driven by campaign contributions in the context of the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission.  Drawing on 10 years worth of letters from legislators to the commission, we find that these letters are filled with specific references to companies (and in some instances the CEOs) they are seeking to assist.  We  match these letters with campaign finance records to see whether Members' efforts are primarily concerned with their donors or their constituents.  
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Concern over the potential for campaign contributions to influence the policy-making process has been a central question in American politics over the last several decades.  Much of the academic study in this area has focused around whether campaign contributions influence legislators' roll call votes.  While these numerous studies have produced somewhat mixed results, on the whole the answer appears to be no: that most of the time campaign contributions don't influence how a member votes in the context of a roll call vote.  While the academic focus on roll call votes is perhaps not surprising given the availability of the data, this precise availability and visibility makes them a poor vehicle for those seeking to quietly influence the policy-making process who may not want to draw scrutiny from journalist or constituents. 

In comparison to the intensity academic scrutiny given to roll call votes, much less attention has been given to congressional letter-marking activity--when legislators write letters to bureaucratic agencies urging them toward or against some regulatory decision.\footnote{This has begun to change in the last few years with recent studies by  \citet{MillsKalafHuges2015,Ritchie2017,Lowande2018JOP,LowandeRitchieLauterbach2018}.}  While technically these letters are subject to federal open records act requirements that process is often slow and laborious, meaning that in practice these letters rarely see the light of day and are highly unlikely to draw public scrutiny.  Thus letter-marking may be a preferred vehicle for those seeking to quietly influence the policy-making process through campaign contributions.  In this paper, we deeply examine one agency -- the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission--and whether campaign contributions to legislators appears to explain whether or not those legislators write letters to the agency on behalf of those companies.  


\section{Arnold's Impact: Studying Congress-Bureaucracy}

We use letter marking activity to study how members of Congress attempt to influence the way bureaucratic appropriation decisions are affected by Congressional intervention.  Arnold's classic book \emph{Congress and the Bureaucracy: A Theory of Influence} deeply influences this project---just as it influences nearly every paper that examines how Congress and the bureaucracy interact to allocate funds.  Like much of Arnold's work \emph{Congress and Bureaucracy} builds a theory of bureauracts and legislators who \emph{anticipate} how others will react to their actions. Members of Congress anticipate how constituents will react to their actions in the institution and bureaucrats anticipate how the allocation decisions that they make will affect members of Congress. 

While the idea that legislators anticipate how their constituents will react is most completely developed in \emph{The Logic of Congressional Action}, \emph{Congress and Bureaucracy} contains the early version of these ideas.  Arnold makes clear that legislators are forward thinking when he argues that ``[f]requently the problem is to anticipate how one's constituents might react in some future election rather than to discern on where they stand on some issue today" \citep[27]{Arnold79}.  Constituency service and the allocation of funds to the district are valuable, Arnold argues, because they serve the needs of reelection.  Not only do the funds allocated to the district provide legislators with the opportunity to impress the direct beneficiary, but they also provide the opportunity for legislators to broadly make claim credit for the allocation of funds to the district.       

In order to create the impression that the legislators were pivotal, Arnold argues, it is key that legislators at least create the impression that they have fought for their constituent's interests.  This can be particularly valuable for moneyed interests who would like to see a return on their donations to elected officials.  Donors who allocate funds to elected officials may be particularly interested in seeing that elected officials will fight for their district---and the donor's---interests with the federal bureaucracy.  As a result, even if the letters have little affect on the allocation of bureaucratic decisions, Arnold's argument in \emph{Congress and the Bureaucracy} suggests that the impression that letters sent from members of Congress to bureaucrats can help legislators cultivate future donations and support in future elections.  Legislators write letters, then, in anticipation of how those the legislator fights for will behave in the future. 

Like legislators who anticipate the responses of key constituents, bureaucrats anticipate how legislators will react to their decisions.  In \emph{Congress and Bureaucracy}, Arnold argues that bureaucrats have distinct (and sometimes competing) goals.  Perhaps not surprisingly, bureaucrats want to maintain---and even expand---their budget.  But this goal can come into competition with their other goals, such as sound public policy.  The literature that examines bureaucratic action has built upon Arnold's idea of bureaucratic anticipation.  For example, theories that argue that bureaucrats cultivate (and then deploy) their reputation fundamentally argue that bureaucrats build this reputation through anticipation of how key constituencies will react to the agencies actions \citep{Carpenter2002}%this is the Carpenter bureaucracy book. 

Even agencies like FERC that are allocated a large amount of statutory independence have reason to be concerned with how members of Congress will react to their decisions.  First, members of Congress could affect the budget of the agency, making it difficult for the agency to regulate key features of the energy market. Second, if FERC drifts too far away from Congressional priorities, then they might reasonably anticipate a statutory revision to their domain.  And third, even in the absence of those drastic actions, bureaucrats could expect that key members of Congress could make it more difficult for the agency to conduct its business through hearings or other investigations. 

In \emph{Congress and the Bureaucracy} Arnold argues that bureaucrats anticipate the reaction of elected officials to the allocation decisions. But the letter marking behavior that we observe can help bureaucrats resolve their informational problem.  Rather than anticipating based on their own knowledge of the district, the letters members of Congress send to the agency can help them understand how regulatory decisions from FERC can affect a legislator's key constituencies in the district.  

Arnold's key insights of anticipation---that actors are forward looking when making decisions, reacting to how the world might be rather than how it is right now---provides the background for understanding why letters to FERC will matter for legislators and for members of Congress alike.  With this notion of anticipation in mind, we now describe the literature on the influence of money in politics.

\subsection{Findings: Previous Research on Influence of Money}

Donors appear to contribute in ways consistent with short term access seeking (literature from Snyder; Powell and Grimmer; Fourinaies and Hall). 

Yet, the evidence that legislators undertake actions explicitly for donors is limited.  There are some exceptions. First, there is the contribution to donations literature.  Where it is clear that corporations donate to pivotal legislators charitable committees.  But this fails to connect to key actions in the institution. Broockman and Kalla (2016) show that donors are more likely to get meetings with key members of Congress.  But again, there is no connection to what legislators are actually doing in the institution.  

Of course, we should have reason to be skeptical of finding this in roll call votes.  (CITATIONS ON HOW THIS HAS FAILED OVER TIME). 

Hall (1996) and subsequent work argues that we have to look at lower levels of Congressional action to detect the influence of corporations.  Building on this logic, subsidy theory of lawmaking suggests that donors will subsidize legislators to take actions that advance legislators explicit goals.   

Letter marking is a place where we could expect to find this influence of campaign donations. In line with Arnold's argument of the anticipated benefits of particularistic spending, legislators already might have incentive to advocate for the interests of within district donors.  Not only will a project authorized by FERC direct financial benefits to the district, it will also provide the elected official with the opportunity to claim credit for the expenditure \citep{Arnold79, GriWesMes14}.  Within the set of projects, then, it is reasonable to expect that elected officials will prioritize those that come from donors, if the projects are otherwise comparable.
